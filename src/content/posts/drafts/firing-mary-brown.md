<p>Mary Beth Brown served Elon Musk as a personal assistant for over twelve years. </p><p>They started working together in SpaceX early days, and she contributed first-person in setting the company culture as the outrageously bold startup was finding its place among the constellation of shooting-star companies.</p><p>Then in early 2014, she asked for a raise.</p><hr><p>According to Ashlee Vance, in his book <em><em>“</em></em><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elon_Musk:_Tesla,_SpaceX,_and_the_Quest_for_a_Fantastic_Future" rel="noopener ugc nofollow"><em><em>Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future</em></em></a><em><em>,”</em></em> Brown asked for a raise, and Musk reacted proposing a simple test: </p><p><em>MB would take 2 weeks off and EM would have a feeling for how valuable her contribution was to the company.</em></p><p>Once the two weeks passed, Musk fired his long-term assistant.</p><hr><p>Now – we could run a few questions on Musk's motives, and we can honestly believe <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2017/08/11/elon-musk-says-he-didnt-fire-his-assistant-when-she-asked-for-a-raise.html#:~:text=as%20company%20complexity%20grew%2C%20the%20role%20required%20several%20specialists%20vs%20one%20generalist">Elon's explanation</a> for the need of multiple specialists to cover Mary Brown's responsibilities. </p><p><strong>What really throws me off is the timing. </strong></p><p>Why test MB's value only in response to her request for a raise? Did she play it wrong, not making sure to be irreplaceable? Must an employee become a bottleneck in order to claim a raise? Or do they have to present a written offer from another firm?</p><p>I have even more philosophical questions here: </p><hr><p><em>Does a raise recognize your current value?</em></p><p><em>Or your past contribution? </em></p><p><em>Or is it an incentive to develop your potential and maximize your contribution to the company? </em></p><hr><p>In the Brown vs Musk case, there was an undeniable contribution that MB brought to SpaceX, Tesla, and Elon Musk personally. And that was probably taken into account in <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2017/08/11/elon-musk-says-he-didnt-fire-his-assistant-when-she-asked-for-a-raise.html#:~:text=MB%20was%20given%2052%20weeks%20of%20salary%20and%20stock%20in%20appreciation%20for%20her%20great%20contribution">her severance package</a>. So her past contribution was recognized.</p><p>By firing her, it was proven beyond any doubt that Musk, or the company, didn't see any potential to be developed any further.</p><p><strong>What throws me off - again - is that Musk wasn't aware of his personal assistant's current value.</strong></p><p>They were working day in day out together, and he had to run a two weeks test to figure out whether she was valuable or not?</p><p>What the hell!</p><hr><p>My takeaway from Mary Brown's experience is simple:</p><p><em><strong>If you need asking for a raise, probably the company doesn't deserve your contribution, for your boss is not aware of the value you bring into the game.</strong></em></p><p>Now this is a bit harsh. I'm a manager myself and I openly admit that I am not aware in real-time of every one of my report's current value.</p><p>That's why – in Denmark – we have yearly salary reviews.</p><p>It's a good and planned opportunity when bosses can – and should – take the proper time to review their report's current value and potential, and propose a matching adjustment to their salary. It also helps keeping the competition off.</p><p>This time of the year is about to come for my company, and I hope my boss reads this article 😎.</p>
