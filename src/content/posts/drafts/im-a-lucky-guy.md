<p>The guy between me and Happiness is my Dark Self, the True Self, obsessed with money, objectified women, and the constant need for external admiration and approval. I'm the good chap, the well-educated, God-feared, play-by-the-rules kid. I'm keeping the monster at bay. Well, most of the time. There are situations in which I must feed it some scraps of me so it can go back to sleep for a little longer. Where did it come from?</p><p>I'm not as lucky as the other guys. I haven't been beaten by my dad when he was coming home drunk in the night. I haven't starved when my single mom couldn't put food on the table after losing her second job. I haven't been bullied because of the color of my skin or my stuttering. Those are lucky guys; they have a clear daemon to blame and to fight. My struggle is opaque; my daemon lurks behind the mist. I have to squeeze my eyes to catch the shape of it, and no matter how hard I try, until now, I couldn't quite catch a clear picture of it. But it is there for me. I can hear it.</p><p>I was born out of an unlikely mix of contrasting genes. My dad is an explosion of poorly managed entrepreneurial power. My mom is the incarnation of a government employee. My dad follows his heart in everything he does; he questions rules, and he challenges the status quo. My mom follows the Word of God, mostly fearing His wrath, flocking along life with all the other sheep. Mom and dad sit on opposite sides of the Book of the Rules. None of them is capable of understanding the other's point of view. Why are they married? I'm still wondering.</p><p>As often happens in Northern Italian families, the man of the house follows his wife's habit, so we are a self-labeled Catholic family. Regularly attending the week's service, always asking God for his majestic forgiveness. Growing up with my dad's curiosity and my mom's fears, it is no wonder that I was constantly in a full-blown sin condition. I don't remember <em>feeling</em> sinful. I <em>knew</em> I was. I felt my parents knew I did sin. I never cared much for God's forgiveness, but I did care enough to hide my sins from my parents. Once God forgives, the sin goes away, right? That was my sound logic as a kid.</p><p>God had always been a doubtful presence in my Life. Ever since they explained to me the concept of an absolute-but-forgiving being, I knew it was <em>BS</em>. But the system, and my mom, managed to instill in me enough fear of eternal damnation as so to keep the doors open to doubt. It's this Doubt that keeps my monster at bay. Without this Doubt, <em>God only knows</em> what kind of man I would be. The Doubt is also the source of my innermost and most disruptive stress. Not knowing is relentlessly killing me.</p><p>I first set foot in a church by myself when I was eight. The local building was still under construction, and the priest held service in a prefabricated structure that was little more than a tent located a few hundred meters from home. Old memories are deceiving and distorted by time and by the meaning that one wants to attribute to them, still, I remember walking up to the priest and start questioning him about the big inconsistencies of the God Theory. Why do people still die? Why do many Gods want to be the Only True God? The kinds of childish questions that are so common among pre-teens. The priest wasn't happy about it, and my next memory is the wrath of my mom (a good approximation to the Wrath of God) that took me back home. I can never start to imagine how embarrassed she felt that day. Her offspring questioning the Absolute Truth in front of the entire neighborhood!</p><p>My mom's only fault was loving her son beyond anything imaginable limit, trying her best to pay forward the family and spiritual values she was taught in her youth—nothing weird, nothing evil. I was just one more kid growing up in a mild Catholic country. Was I born in Poland or Ireland... I'm not sure I would have survived that fate. Not with my damned curiosity. I mildly blame my father, though. I can't recall him standing up to my mom, protecting me from the religious <em>BS</em>. Ever. He never expressed his own opinions on the matter, but even as a child, I could tell he was on my side. Still, I didn't receive the support I knew was there. But again, that's how things used to play out in Italy in the '80. It was society, not personal calls. I love mom and dad.</p><p>I'm overweight; I've always been edging obesity. I blame it on the constitution, but I know that I've always eaten candies and junk food. Calories don't lie. My weight powers my demons. I've always been ashamed of my body, but I never understood why. It's always been a shame that comes from the outside. Like many chubby kids, I got bullied for my shape. Nothing worth a movie, though. They called me names and a little more. I was lucky never to meet real assholes in my life. I was lucky not to have to face social media. My shame comes from mom and dad. My mom always hid sweets at home, halving my portions and constantly complaining that blue jeans meant for my age didn't fit; she had to buy pants made for older kids and pay for the shortening service. Anytime money was involved, it was a massive deal for my mom. My father wasn't as persistent with his remarks. As a matter of fact, I only remember two episodes in which my dad referred to my weight, but they sting today as they did back then. On one occasion, I spent my pocket money and bought sweets for my friends and me. We biked up the hill to hide away and eat them all. Even back in the days, I tried to lower my shame by sharing the guilt, unconsciously using social events to justify my cravings for junk food. Our hideaway happened to be precisely on my father's way back from work. He caught me literally with my hands in the jelly. I don't recall him saying much. He just took me home, away from my friends, with no reason offered on the spot. He let my mom play the blame card. To this day, I still don't know whether my mom was angry because of me eating sweets or because I squandered my pocket money. The second episode happened in my early teens. I may have been twelve years old when my father gathered me in the courtyard and, referring to our obese neighbor, asked: <em>"would you like her to be your mom?"</em>. That shocked me as I immediately fear the "divorce" thing, for my parents were fighting a lot back then. But I was wrong. Oh boy I was wrong. After me saying <em>"no dad, I don't like her"</em>, he promptly continued on my assist: <em>"that is because nobody likes fat people"</em>. And with that piece of wisdom cast upon his fragile son, he walked away. This may easily have been the origin of my objectification of the female body. I seek perfection in others because I can't find it within myself.</p><p>My dad grew up in a poor family in the aftermath of WWII. There was much to rebuild and little money going around. He had to use a spoon with holes to eat breakfast. This way, he could eat hard bread soaked in milk, but most of the substance would pour back into the bowl to be reheated the next day. He lived hungrily, and he must have had strong and conflicting feelings regarding his spoiled son, that was growing up to be overweight. On the bright side, my weight clearly showed him providing for his family. But he still had a fat firstborn. That must have stung.</p><p>Money is at least as conflicting as God. Money is the big dichotomy of my mother, and I grew up in such a contrasting situation that I've ended up not understanding the value of money. To this day, I don't get what money is. And I end up wasting it. Mom was preaching the False God: money. Beware of money! beware, the False God! A good man lives in poverty. Look at the life of the saints. Look at S. Francesco's life; he gave up all his earthly riches to walk barefoot, praying to God. I never got that choice. That was my mom on a Sunday after-church religious sprint. On regular days money was never enough. We didn't have enough money to compare to our family and friends. My dad spent his life chasing after money the only way he knew: working his ass from morning to night, over and over again. But my dad's income was never good enough for my mom, who lived in awe and envy of her friend Giandomenico. He was the man with money. They lived in a big house; he knew how to manage money. The man was making money <em>AND</em> spending time with his family while my father was failing on both fronts. The "Giandomenico" word was a ubiquitous presence in my parent's fights. My father couldn't match Giandomenico the same way I couldn't match the heights of my cousin Michele. In my mom's eyes, they were better than us, and it didn't matter what dad and I tried to do to please her expectations. We could not win hour fights. We could never stop fighting. Endlessly.</p><p>My father is a great engineer. Not by any school title. He is intelligent and curious enough to look at things, find out how they work, and either fix or improve them. He was forced into a hard work career as a plumber by his father at age thirteen. He was not offered education; he could not choose as I did. He started working, and that was it. And he was good at that. My dad - now retired - was great at his job. He was better than anybody else, and customers could tell. He was successful. But entrepreneurial success begets paperwork, and that is something my dad and I are terrible at. Instead of trusting his wife with the economics of the company, he trusted other people. And people took advantage of his ingenuity and trust.  My dad could have made it big but ended up with close to nothing because he wasn't able - or didn't put the due attention - to paperwork. I am at risk of following the same path. Fortunately for me, it is clear that I should not run my own company. So the mistakes that I make with bills and dues are smaller by orders of magnitude.</p><p>In my family, Money was never enough, but there never was a goal for what "enough" might be. There wasn't enough money, and that's it. That set my career flywheel in motion. Over twenty years, I've moved from job to job, constantly improving, always studying, and always striving to be the best. I achieved results, and I have a great career going on.</p><p>Nevertheless, it is never enough. My results are never good enough. I always feel I need to prove myself more. I always see other people having it better than me. But I don't have a set goal. I don't know how to create one.</p>
