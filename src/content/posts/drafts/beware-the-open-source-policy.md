<p>My company recently introduced an Open-Source Policy that can be summarised as follows:</p><ul><li>Use OS software only if distributed with an MIT license</li><li>Small OS projects must be internalized</li><li>No contribution to OS is allowed</li></ul><p>At first, that struck me as a blow below the belt, I got on the edge of tech depression, and I immediately considered quitting such a company, for I strongly believe in Open-Source.</p><p>But after a few days and one sleepless night, I realized this approach is good for the OS Community. Maybe, it's just not the right place for my idealistic mindset. Or maybe it is.</p><h2 id="thy-shall-open-source-or-die">Thy shall Open-Source (or die)</h2><p>Some companies adopt a liberal Open-Source Policy, like <a href="https://buffer.com/resources/guide-open-source/">Buffer</a>. </p><p>These companies become active contributors to the community, and a few are likely to attract like-minded talents and make it big in the tech news circle, conferences and book. Many die trying.</p><p>By pushing new OS software into the community, they provide value, but they also inevitably inject bugs and issues that need time and resources to solve. </p><p>Of course, they also work and fix those stuff. <br><em>Give'm time, man!</em></p><blockquote>My observation is that those companies <strong>tend to deal with low-risk businesses</strong> where such defects can do little-to-no harm.</blockquote><p>Nobody gets hurt if a comment on a Facebook post gets lost. <br>Its author will re-write it and send some good thoughts to Mark.</p><h2 id="the-risk-that-you-dont-want-to-take">The risk that &lt;YOU&gt; don't want to take </h2><p>But what if the same bug was on a trading platform? What if your payment to the el-bill gets lost? What if your salary gets lost? What if your accounting software gives you the wrong number?</p><p>You, me, your mom, her uncle... none of us want to get harmed when it comes to the universally shared aspects of our lives:</p><ul><li>health</li><li>money &amp; taxes</li></ul><p>Companies in such areas <strong>can't take the same risk level </strong>as a Tinder-like does.</p><blockquote>My observation is that when you sit on a jetplane, you tend to hope that they didn't use the library that you pushed on GitHub last Sunday... without testing it... because it was Sunday.</blockquote><p>Those Companies tend to adopt an Open-Source Policy similar to the one at the beginning of this article. They want well-proven OS only, or they want to control the source of it to run risk-management assessments.</p><h2 id="two-sides-of-the-same-coin">Two sides of the same coin</h2><p>Is one of the two approaches better than the other? No.</p><blockquote><em>IMHO</em>:<br>both are needed for the Open-Source Community to thrive.</blockquote><p>I call companies like Buffer (I'm not affiliated with them)  <strong>the "Horses."</strong> They lead the OS Community by example; they produce new ideas, disrupt existing approaches and create innovation.</p><p>I call companies like TeamSystem, <strong>the "Drovers."</strong> They invest money in evaluating consolidated alternatives. When they choose NGiNX over Apache, they don't express just a preference: <strong>they share with the world their evaluation and the money they spent in running it</strong>.</p><blockquote class="kg-blockquote-alt">A (wild) Horse can not provide value without a Drover that chooses their work. Open-Source can not survive without adopter-only companies.</blockquote><p>It is also important to underline that <em>Drovers</em> companies participate in the Open-Source Community by purchasing consultancy services that are often the only business model behind <em>Horses</em> companies.</p><p>You can do the same and participate in a new product development any time you pledge a crazy idea on Kickstarter!</p><h2 id="are-you-working-in-the-wrong-place">Are you working in the wrong place?</h2><p>If you, as a person, tend to identify with the <em>Horse</em>, you may feel that a natural match for you is a <em>Horse</em> company. It's not a given, though.</p><p>I work for a <em>Drover</em> company that deals with accounting and taxes, and I feel like a mad horse that is kept from running as fast as he possibly can. </p><p>The frustration is high, and last week I reached my tipping point when my company decided to internalize a piece of OS software that I'm proud of maintaining. </p><p>My sight went red, I could not work, and I could not sleep: I felt betrayed that such a big money-making company decided to steal away from the OS instead of contributing to it.</p><p>My tech-soul-less boss had no possible way to emphasize with me, nor could my wife. They actually think very much alike and told me the same thing over and over: let it go.</p><p>But when you are passionate about a subject, you can't let it go. You must get to the bottom of things, you need to understand.</p><p>A <em>Horse engineer</em> that works for a <em>Drover</em> company can influence the Open-Source Community by sponsoring a Horse-oriented mindset in low(er) risk areas of the company business.</p><p>For example, in my company, the Frontend side of tech has a low risk compared to the calculation algorithms used in the Backend. I can push to move towards a <em>Horse</em> approach for the Frontend while we keep a <em>Drover</em> approach in the backend.</p><h2 id="are-you-safe-working-there">Are you safe working there?</h2><p>Internalizing Open-Source software, provided that is MIT licensed, is no legal problem... unless the author of such software is also an employee. </p><p>In such a case, my case, the problem is a possible IP infringement legal action that my company can undertake in case the relationship goes sour.</p><p>So far, I have worked on my OS libraries in my spare time (which is already a risk for me) and devoted my attention to company stuff during company time.</p><p>Once my library gets internalized, I will be in pure conflict for any change I make to the internal version of the library will be company IP that, in theory, I can not push into the OS version.</p><p>So, how can this work out? Should I quit and avoid finding out? Should I trust the company and risk it?</p><p>I haven't decided yet. My tech-soul-less but rather resourceful boss promised to help find a legal way to manage this issue. </p><p>If we find out, I'll share it in the name of Open-Source knowledge 🤠!</p>
