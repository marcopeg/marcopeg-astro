<p>The job was to build the corporate website for a hot-shot Italian piano producer. The look and feel of the final product were paramount – and it was 2008 – so <strong>the go-to tech for cool-looking web UI was Flash</strong>.</p><p>I got into the freelance gig for half of the project, as most of the <em>CMS</em> was pure web-based, but <strong>it had to integrate a big Flash header</strong> full of movements and cool-looking menus. I even considered giving back the down payment and quitting the gig. For I wasn't able to effectively communicate with my client.</p><blockquote>Fortunately, my ex wife came to rescue. </blockquote><hr><h3 id="right-at-the-peak-of-the-heat">Right at the peak of the heat</h3><p><strong>She suggested I reverse the pressure</strong> by constantly <strong>feeding my customer with status updates</strong>, clarifying all the progress that I was making, and visualizing the bottleneck that I was experiencing with the <em>Flash</em> part.</p><p>Above all, she suggested I <strong>ask my client's help in improving the communication </strong>with the famous design firm, simply pointing out that I didn't have enough weight to be heard by them. updates <br>2) ask my client's help with the Flash's firm</blockquote><hr><h3 id="that-morning-i-started-to-compose-a-status-update-email">That morning, I started to compose a status update email. </h3><p>I would send that email every morning and every afternoon for the rest of the project.</p><p><em>Every morning</em>, I would <strong>share with my client the plan for the day</strong> and the steps that I expected to accomplish within the scope of the project. That served to <strong>create a small expectation in my customer's mind</strong>. </p><p>I would also <strong>point out my expectations</strong> on the Flash's firm deliveries, effectively sharing such expectations with my client.</p><p>In the late afternoon, I would send a daily report on what had been done. In that report, I was keen to <strong>list some unexpected and positive results</strong> on my end. That served me well in <strong>establishing a solid trust</strong> between me and my client. </p><p>Whenever my <em>Flash</em> counterpart did not deliver <strong>I would not point fingers</strong> at them. I would simply share the information, asking my client whether they received any intel that could help me understand how to proceed with the shared goal.</p><blockquote>The trick was to continuosly ask for my customer's help.</blockquote><hr><h3 id="in-just-a-few-days-my-clients-tone-of-voice-changed">In just a few days my client's tone of voice changed. </h3><p>They started to ask for information instead of spitting blame on me. They were interested in <strong>what more could I do for them</strong>, in case the <em>Flash</em> part was not to be delivered. They opened up and shared their frustration and uncertainties towards the other firm.</p><p>I ended up <strong>gainig a bigger gig</strong> to implement the visual part using <em>Web Technologies</em>. Back then, it meant building some nice transition effects with <em>jQuery</em> and <em>CSS</em>. For a mid-level engineer, <strong>it was a dream project!</strong></p>
